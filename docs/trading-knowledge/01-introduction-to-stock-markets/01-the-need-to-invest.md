## 1.1 – Overview

Imagine this – you wake up one fine morning, get ready, and go to work like any other day. However, today is different; your HR manager informs you that the company has decided to lay you off. Feeling sorry for your situation, your manager hands you a nice severance package which includes a lump sum cheque of Rs.500,000/-.

For a moment, you feel special. After all, you have in your hand a cheque for half a million rupees! A quick analysis later, you realize a few compelling facts –

- You are really not sure when you can get a job again
- You have a certain amount of monthly obligations to meet, i.e., school fees, utility bills, phone bills, groceries, medical expenses, etc.
- Assuming your monthly obligation is Rs.25,000/- per month, the money in your hand is expected to last for about 500,000/25,000 = 20 months, after which you will be broke if you don't find another job

These thoughts put you in a panic mode and suddenly that half a million rupees does not seem as much!

For a moment, let us put aside this grim and depressing story and take a different point of view. Let us assume you have a magic wand that would allow you to solve this problem. How would you? What can you do to ensure Rs.500,000/- last really long, maybe for your entire life?

Think about it carefully.

What if there was a way you could actually do this without any magic?

Historically speaking, great investors have managed to generate wealth by investing their money for a long period of time. It would certainly be a great skill to learn how to put your money to good use, especially if this would protect you in a crisis. After all, what else would you do with your dormant money? For a moment, think about the Rs.500,000/- – this is your money and it is just lying with you, without really serving any purpose. Instead, you could smartly deploy this and earn a return on this money.

Deploying money with an expectation of making more money is "Investing". When you invest, you give your money a purpose.

## 1.2 – A bit of History

To give you a sense of the power of equity investments, allow me to cite a bit of history.

There are many examples across the world where equity investment has created enormous wealth over a long period of time. But what is the actual return we are talking about here? Let us check the Indian equity market's performance.

India has two major domestic stock exchanges – The National Stock Exchange (NSE) and the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE). The BSE has completed nearly 140 years of its existence, and NSE has completed about 25 years. A bellwether index represents the broader market performance.

The Sensex is the BSE's market index consisting of 30 well-established companies listed in the BSE. The Sensex was trading at 100 points on 1st April 1979. Today (as of 19th Jan 2015), the Sensex is trading at 28,100 – translating to a whopping 281x return over the last 35 years!

To get a perspective of this, imagine you invest just Rs.10,000/- on 1st April 1979 in the equity markets. Thanks to the 281x return generated by the markets, this investment would be valued at Rs.28,10,000/- today!

This is truly a wealth compounder. In fact, if you extend the time frame, the wealth generated would even be higher. Check the table below that shows the wealth created (based on Sensex's value) for different time periods.

The table clearly shows that the wealth created gets bigger and better as the time frame increases. Historically there is a very strong correlation between equity investment and time – the more time you give to your investments, the more you stand to gain!

The reasons for this are actually simple:

**The Rule of Compounding** – Albert Einstein once said, "Compound interest is the eighth wonder of the world. He who understands it, earns it…he who doesn't, pays it". Compounding is a simple, time-tested mathematical phenomenon that makes money work for you. To give you an example, assume a certain sum of money, say Rs.10,000/-, is growing at 20% year on year basis (recall the investment in Sensex table above). This means at the end of the 1st year, Rs.10,000/- becomes Rs.12,000/-. We get Rs.12,000/- by way of adding interest of Rs.2,000/- to the initial capital of Rs.10,000/-. Now in the 2nd year, we earn an interest of Rs.2,400/- on Rs.12,000/-, and we get Rs.14,400/-. In the 3rd year, we earn an interest of Rs.2,880/-, and we get Rs.17,280/-. So and so forth. The money grows exponentially. The longer we stay invested, the harder our money works for us.

Also, by virtue of investing early, you stay invested for a long period of time. This means you give yourself more time to let the compounding magic work for you.

## 1.3 – Things to note before investing

Before investing, it is essential to ensure a few basic things. One must have:

1. **A steady source of income** – Salaries, income from business, pension, rent, etc.
2. **Excess cash** – In other words, cash that you can afford to lose. This is the cash that is left after settling all the monthly obligations like rent, bills, insurance premium, shopping expenses, etc.
3. **Emergency fund** – This is a specific sum of money set aside for dealing with emergencies like a health issue or sudden unemployment. On average, one should set aside at least 6 months' worth of expenses to deal with such emergencies.

If the above three things are in place, you are all set to invest!

Finally, you would need a reasonable investment goal. Investment goals are specific financial objectives. Here are a few examples of possible financial goals:

- Planning an overseas vacation worth Rs.7,50,000/- three years from now
- Retirement corpus of Rs.5,00,00,000/- upon retirement which is 20 years away
- Down payment of Rs.10,00,000/- for a house in 5 years
- Children's education of Rs.15,00,000/- which is 8 years away

So on and so forth.

It is important to have a realistic investment goal as this will drive you to achieve the goal. To accomplish this, you need to invest your money in the right asset class which has the potential to create wealth.

## 1.4 – The assets classes

You are familiar with the word "Asset" by now. Simply put, an asset is a resource with an economic value that an individual owns or controls with the expectation that it will provide a future benefit.

There are several assets like equity, real estate, fixed income instruments, mutual funds, etc. We will understand each of these:

**Fixed income instruments** – Fixed income instruments (also referred to as fixed deposits) are deposits made at banks or NBFCs like HDFC Ltd, LIC Housing Finance, Bajaj FinServ, etc. These are relatively safer instruments to invest in. The money you invest in these instruments will earn you an interest which is paid along with the principal upon maturity. The fixed income instruments have a fixed timeline. Upon maturity, the money is returned along with a pre-specified interest rate.

**Gold/Silver** – These are precious metals. Investments in gold and silver are considered safe havens. Especially in India, gold investments are considered very auspicious. You can invest in gold and silver either in the form of jewelry, exchange-traded funds (ETF), or the sovereign gold bonds issued by the Reserve Bank of India.

**Real Estate** – Real estate is a popular investment option for Indians. A real estate investment involves transacting (buying and selling) commercial and non-commercial land. Typical examples include transacting in sites, apartments, commercial buildings, and agricultural lands.

**Equity (Stocks)** – The equity asset class is an investment made by purchasing shares of publicly listed corporations. Shares are listed and traded on stock exchanges like BSE and NSE. Equities offer the best risk-reward ratio among all assets.

**Commodities** – A commodity is a basic good used in commerce that is interchangeable with other commodities of the same type. Typical examples are crude oil, wheat, cotton, precious metals, etc. Commodities are traded on dedicated commodity exchanges.

Now the decision to invest in any asset class depends on the risk and return characteristics of the asset.

## 1.5 – Return and Risk

When you invest in an asset, the expectation is to generate wealth over the long term. The instrument you invest in should generate a return good enough to beat the average inflation rate of the economy.

For example – in India, the average inflation rate is about 4-5%. This means goods and services that cost Rs.100/- today will cost you Rs.104 or Rs.105 or even more a year from now. So if we invest Rs.100/- somewhere, it is only reasonable for us to expect that investment to grow to Rs.105 or above a year from now.

For every asset, there are two sources of return:

1. **The annual yield** – This is income the asset generates. For instance, for fixed-income instruments, the return comes from interest paid. For equities, it comes from the dividends.
2. **Capital appreciation** – Capital appreciation is the change in asset price. This is also called the capital gain. For example, if you buy an asset at Rs.100/- and sell it for Rs.120/-, then the capital appreciation is Rs.20/-.

The sum of the annual yield and the capital appreciation is called the "Absolute Return". For example, assume you purchase an asset for Rs.1,00,000/-. This asset generates an annual yield of 2%, which is about Rs.2,000/-, and by the end of the year, the asset price goes up by Rs.5,000/-. This translates to an absolute return of Rs.7,000/- (2,000 + 5,000).

The percentage return is simply the absolute return divided by the investment amount. In this case, it would be:

Percentage return = 7000/100000 = 7%

This is a simple single-period return. It is called a single-period return because we looked at just 1 year. When we deal with multiple periods, calculating the return can get a bit tricky, which we will learn later in the module.

The important point to note here is that there is a **trade-off between risk and return**. The higher the expected return, the higher the expected risk. The lower the return, the lower the risk. The trick is to build a portfolio keeping in mind your risk appetite and return requirements.

Equities, as an asset class, offer the best risk-reward ratio – meaning the expected risk to the expected return is well balanced. This is the reason why investors prefer to invest in equity as an asset class.

## Key Takeaways

1. Invest to make your money work for you
2. The three basic things you need to start investing are:
   - Steady source of income
   - Surplus funds
   - Emergency fund set aside
3. Have a realistic investment goal
4. Asset Classes in India – Fixed Income, Equity, Commodities, Gold/Silver, and Real Estate
5. Risk and return are directly related
6. Equities offer the best risk-reward ratio
