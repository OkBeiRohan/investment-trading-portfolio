## 4.1 – Why do companies go public?

A company goes public for several reasons. The most common reason is to raise money for growth and expansion. When a company is in its early stages, it typically has limited funds. As it grows, it needs more capital to:

- Expand operations
- Enter new markets
- Invest in research and development
- Acquire other companies
- Pay off debts
- Provide an exit route for early investors

Let's understand this through an example.

## 4.2 – Origin of a Business

Imagine a young techie who has developed innovative photo editing software. Let's call him Ajay. Ajay believes his software has great potential and wants to commercialize it. However, he needs money to:

- Hire employees
- Set up an office
- Market the product
- Build infrastructure

Ajay estimates he needs about Rs. 10,00,000/- (Rs. 10 lakhs) to get started. He has Rs. 3,00,000/- in savings. Where can he get the remaining Rs. 7,00,000/-?

**Option 1: Borrow from friends and family** – Ajay could borrow from people he knows. However, this has limitations. His social circle may not have the required capital, and borrowing from friends/family can strain relationships if the business fails.

**Option 2: Loan from a bank** – Ajay could approach a bank for a business loan. However, as a startup with no track record, getting a loan is difficult. Even if he gets one, the interest burden will eat into profits.

**Option 3: Angel Investor** – An angel investor is an individual who provides capital to startups in exchange for equity (ownership stake). Angel investors typically invest in early-stage companies with high growth potential.

Ajay decides to approach an angel investor. Let's call the investor Arjun.

### Scene 1: Angel Investment

Arjun evaluates Ajay's business plan and likes it. He agrees to invest Rs. 7,00,000/- in exchange for 30% ownership of the company.

Here's how the ownership structure looks:

- **Ajay's Contribution**: Rs. 3,00,000/- for 70% ownership
- **Arjun's Contribution**: Rs. 7,00,000/- for 30% ownership
- **Total Capital**: Rs. 10,00,000/-

The company's **valuation** is calculated as:

Total Capital / Total Outstanding Shares = Price per Share

If the company issues 10,000 shares:

- Ajay owns: 7,000 shares (70%)
- Arjun owns: 3,000 shares (30%)

Share price = Rs. 10,00,000 / 10,000 = Rs. 100 per share

This Rs. 100 is called the **Face Value** of the share.

### Scene 2: Building the Business

With Rs. 10 lakhs, Ajay successfully builds and launches his software. Within a year, the business grows significantly:

- Revenue: Rs. 35 lakhs
- Profit: Rs. 6 lakhs
- Customer base: Growing rapidly

Now Ajay wants to expand further. He needs Rs. 25 lakhs for:

- Hiring more developers
- Expanding to new markets
- Increasing marketing efforts

### Scene 3: Venture Capital (Series A Funding)

Ajay approaches a Venture Capital (VC) firm for funding. VCs are professional investment firms that invest larger amounts in high-growth companies in exchange for equity.

The VC firm evaluates the business and makes an offer:

- **Investment**: Rs. 25 lakhs
- **Equity Given**: 14% of the company

But wait – how is this 14% calculated?

The VC firm has valued Ajay's company at Rs. 1.75 crores (Rs. 1,75,00,000). Here's the math:

If 14% = Rs. 25,00,000
Then 100% = Rs. 25,00,000 / 0.14 = Rs. 1,78,57,143

(Approximately Rs. 1.75 crores after rounding)

After this investment, the shareholding pattern changes:

- **Ajay**: 70% × (100% - 14%) = 60.2%
- **Arjun (Angel)**: 30% × (100% - 14%) = 25.8%
- **VC Firm**: 14%

The company now has:

- Initial Capital: Rs. 10 lakhs
- Series A Funding: Rs. 25 lakhs
- **Total Capital**: Rs. 35 lakhs

### Scene 4: More Growth, More Funding (Series B, Series C)

As the company continues growing, it may raise additional rounds of funding:

- **Series B**: For scaling operations
- **Series C**: For expansion, maybe acquiring competitors
- **Pre-IPO Round**: Just before going public

Each funding round dilutes existing shareholders' percentages but increases the company's valuation.

Let's say after Series B and Series C:

- Company Valuation: Rs. 100 crores
- Ajay's Stake: 44% (diluted from 60.2%)
- Arjun's Stake: 18% (diluted from 25.8%)
- VC Firm: 10% (diluted from 14%)
- Series B Investors: 16%
- Series C Investors: 12%

### Scene 5: Initial Public Offering (IPO)

After several years of growth, Ajay's company is now worth Rs. 500 crores. The management decides to go public by launching an IPO. Here's why:

**Reasons for IPO:**

1. **Liquidity for existing investors** – Angel investors, VCs, and even Ajay can sell some shares and book profits
2. **Raise capital** – Company can raise money from the general public for further expansion
3. **Brand visibility** – Being listed on stock exchange increases credibility
4. **Employee benefits** – Can offer ESOPs (Employee Stock Option Plans)
5. **Acquisition currency** – Can use shares to acquire other companies

**The IPO Process:**

Let's say the company decides to:

- Offer 20% of the company to the public
- At an IPO price of Rs. 300 per share
- Total shares: 1,00,00,000 (1 crore shares)
- Shares offered in IPO: 20,00,000 shares (20 lakhs)

Money raised from IPO = 20,00,000 × Rs. 300 = Rs. 60 crores

After the IPO:

- **Ajay**: 35.2% (44% reduced to 35.2% after 20% dilution)
- **Arjun**: 14.4%
- **VC Firm**: 8%
- **Series B Investors**: 12.8%
- **Series C Investors**: 9.6%
- **Public (via IPO)**: 20%

The company is now **publicly listed** on the stock exchange (NSE/BSE).

## 4.3 – Understanding Face Value vs Market Price

There's often confusion between face value and market price. Let's clarify:

**Face Value** – This is the original value at which shares were issued when the company was incorporated. It's usually Rs. 1, Rs. 2, Rs. 5, or Rs. 10. Face value is used for accounting purposes and calculating dividends.

**Market Price** – This is the current price at which the share is trading on the stock exchange. It fluctuates based on demand and supply.

For example:

- Infosys: Face Value = Rs. 5, Current Market Price = Rs. 1,400+
- TCS: Face Value = Rs. 1, Current Market Price = Rs. 3,500+

The market price is determined by company performance, investor sentiment, industry trends, and overall market conditions.

## 4.4 – Types of Companies

Before we go further, let's understand types of companies:

**Private Limited Company** – A company that has restrictions on ownership transfer and cannot offer shares to the general public. Denoted by "Pvt Ltd" or "Private Limited" suffix.

**Public Limited Company** – A company that can offer shares to the general public and has no restrictions on share transfer. Denoted by "Ltd" or "Limited" suffix.

**Listed Company** – A public limited company whose shares are listed and traded on stock exchanges.

**Unlisted Company** – A public limited company whose shares are not listed on stock exchanges.

A company must first be a public limited company before it can get listed on stock exchanges.

## 4.5 – Key Terminologies

**Promoters** – The founders and key management who started the company

**Institutional Investors** – Large entities like mutual funds, insurance companies, pension funds, foreign institutional investors (FIIs)

**Retail Investors** – Individual investors like you and me

**HNI (High Net Worth Individual)** – Individuals with investable surplus exceeding Rs. 2 crores

**Anchor Investors** – Large institutional investors who invest before the IPO opens to public

**Book Building** – Process of price discovery where the final IPO price is determined based on investor bids

**Grey Market** – Unofficial market where IPO shares are traded before they're officially listed

**Lot Size** – Minimum number of shares you can apply for in an IPO (usually in multiples of 10-15 shares)

---

## Key Takeaways

1. Companies go through multiple funding stages: Bootstrapping → Angel → Series A (VC) → Series B → Series C → IPO
2. Each funding round dilutes existing shareholders but increases company valuation
3. Face value is the original share value; market price is the current trading price
4. IPO allows companies to raise capital from the public and provides liquidity to early investors
5. Private limited companies must convert to public limited before going public
6. Angel investors and VCs play crucial roles in funding early-stage companies
