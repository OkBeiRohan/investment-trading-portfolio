## 3.1 – Overview

In the previous chapter, we understood the need for a regulatory authority and how it tries to form a level playing field for all the market participants involved. In this chapter, we will understand the market participants involved in the day-to-day functioning of the stock markets.

## 3.2 – The Stock Broker

Think about this – you want to buy a laptop, you go to an authorized dealer and buy one. You want to buy a pair of denims, you go to an apparel store and buy one. But, what if you want to buy shares? Where will you buy that? Perhaps from the stock market, but where and how? Think about it.

The exchange market is not like a typical supermarket where you can walk in and buy things. For one, it does not have a physical location where buyers and sellers can meet and transact. Therefore, there is a need for someone who can act as an intermediary between the buyer and the seller.

A stockbroker is a corporate entity that acts as an intermediary between you (the buyer/seller) and the stock exchange.

The stockbroker facilitates your transactions by providing you access to the exchange. For his services, the stockbroker charges you a fee called the 'brokerage charge'. In India, anyone who wishes to become a stockbroker must seek permission from SEBI and register as a stockbroker.

Zerodha, for example, is a stockbroker. We are members of both the NSE and BSE. We help people to buy and sell shares in the market.

Think about a stockbroker like a mobile phone operator. You cannot talk to your friend using the mobile phone without a telecom operator like Airtel or Vodafone. Likewise, you cannot buy or sell shares without a stockbroker.

There are two types of stockbrokers in India:

1. **Full-Service Brokers** – These brokers offer a wide range of services including research, advisory, investment banking, etc., along with broking. They typically charge higher brokerage. Examples: ICICI Direct, Kotak Securities, Sharekhan, etc.

2. **Discount Brokers** – These brokers offer only basic broking services at a much lower cost. They don't provide advisory or research services. Examples: Zerodha, Upstox, 5Paisa, etc.

## 3.3 – The Depository and Depository Participant (DP)

Imagine you buy 10 shares of Reliance Industries Limited (RIL) from NSE. Once you buy it, the shares have to be credited to your account. In older times (before 1996), the exchange would issue you a share certificate, which was a physical certificate stating your ownership. The share certificate was issued to you in paper format. Every time you sold or bought shares, you had to handle physical share certificates.

This had several limitations:

- **Wear and Tear** – Paper certificates could get damaged
- **Theft and Loss** – Physical certificates could get lost or stolen
- **Forgery** – Fake certificates could be created
- **Transfer Delay** – Physical transfer of certificates took time
- **Storage** – You needed safe storage for certificates

To overcome these limitations, the concept of 'Dematerialization' was introduced in 1996. Dematerialization is the process of converting physical share certificates into electronic format.

Think about it like this – remember the good old days of audio cassettes? We used to carefully handle them, store them, and sometimes they would get stuck in the player. Then came MP3s and digital audio. Music got converted to digital format. Something similar happened with shares.

When shares are held in demat (dematerialized) format, it means they are held electronically in your account. This account is called a 'Demat Account', and it is maintained by a 'Depository'.

In India, there are two depositories:

1. **National Securities Depository Limited (NSDL)** – Started in 1996, NSDL was India's first depository
2. **Central Depository Services Limited (CDSL)** – Started in 1999, CDSL is India's second depository

Just like how banks hold your money in electronic format, depositories hold your shares in electronic format.

Now, as a regular investor, you cannot directly interact with a depository. You need an intermediary, which is called a 'Depository Participant' or DP.

**Depository Participant (DP)** – A DP is an agent of the Depository. They are the intermediary between you and the depository. Banks, stockbrokers, and financial institutions can become DPs after registering with SEBI.

For example, Zerodha is a DP for both CDSL and NSDL. When you open an account with Zerodha, you get both a trading account (for buying/selling) and a demat account (for holding shares electronically).

Your Demat Account is identified by a unique number:

- **For NSDL** – It's a 16-digit number starting with 'IN' (Example: IN301234567890123)
- **For CDSL** – It's a 16-digit numeric number (Example: 1234567890123456)

## 3.4 – Bank

Banks play a very important role in the stock market ecosystem. Whenever you wish to buy shares, you need money in your trading account. Likewise, whenever you sell shares, the money from your sale is credited to your bank account.

For this purpose, you need to link your bank account with your trading and demat account. This is called a 'Bank Account Link'. Every time you buy shares, money is debited from your bank account. Every time you sell shares, money is credited to your bank account.

The transfer of money between your bank account and trading account happens through the bank's payment gateway. The entire process is seamless and electronic, usually happening within a few seconds.

In India, most major banks like HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, SBI, Axis Bank, Kotak Mahindra Bank, etc., are integrated with stock brokers' systems for smooth fund transfers.

## 3.5 – National Securities Clearing Corporation Limited (NSCCL)

Imagine you buy shares from the stock exchange. The shares get debited from the seller's demat account and credited to your demat account. Simultaneously, money gets debited from your bank account and credited to the seller's bank account.

But how does this happen seamlessly? How does the exchange ensure that the buyer gets his shares and the seller gets his money? This is where the 'Clearing Corporation' comes in.

**National Securities Clearing Corporation Limited (NSCCL)** is a clearing and settlement agency for trades executed on the NSE. It acts as a central counterparty to all trades on NSE.

Here's how it works:

1. When you buy shares, you don't know who the seller is
2. When you sell shares, you don't know who the buyer is
3. NSCCL sits in between and guarantees the trade
4. NSCCL ensures that you get your shares when you buy
5. NSCCL ensures that the seller gets his money when he sells

Similarly, for BSE, there is **Indian Clearing Corporation Limited (ICCL)**.

NSCCL and ICCL also perform 'Risk Management' functions. They calculate margins, collect margins from brokers, and ensure that the system remains safe and secure.

## 3.6 – Maintenance Charges

When you open a trading and demat account, there are certain charges involved:

1. **Account Opening Charges** – One-time fee charged when you open an account. Zerodha charges ₹0 for account opening.

2. **Annual Maintenance Charges (AMC)** – Yearly charges for maintaining your trading and demat account. This covers the cost of maintaining records, sending statements, and other operational costs. Different brokers charge different AMC. Zerodha charges ₹300 per year.

3. **Brokerage Charges** – Charges levied on each transaction. These vary widely:
   - Full-service brokers: Typically 0.3% to 1% of transaction value
   - Discount brokers: Typically ₹10 to ₹20 per trade or 0.01% to 0.05%

4. **DP Charges** – Charges for transferring shares from your demat account (when you sell). CDSL and NSDL levy these charges. Zerodha charges ₹13.5 + GST per sell transaction.

5. **Statutory Charges** – These include:
   - **Securities Transaction Tax (STT)** – Tax levied by the government on all transactions
   - **Stamp Duty** – State tax on share transactions
   - **SEBI Turnover Charges** – Charges collected by SEBI
   - **Exchange Transaction Charges** – Charges levied by NSE/BSE
   - **GST** – Goods and Services Tax on brokerage and other charges

All brokers must clearly disclose their charges. You can check the brokerage calculator to understand the exact charges.

## 3.7 – Trading Account vs Demat Account

Many people confuse these two accounts. Let's understand the difference:

**Trading Account:**

- Used for placing buy/sell orders
- Linked to your bank account
- Facilitates transactions
- Provided by stockbroker

**Demat Account:**

- Used for holding shares electronically
- Shares are credited/debited here
- Acts like an electronic locker
- Provided by DP

Think of it like shopping online:

- **Trading Account** = Shopping cart (where you place orders)
- **Demat Account** = Warehouse (where products are stored)
- **Bank Account** = Payment wallet (from where money is debited)

All three work together to complete your transaction.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. Stockbrokers act as intermediaries between you and the stock exchange
2. There are two types of brokers: Full-service brokers and Discount brokers
3. Shares are held in electronic format in a Demat Account
4. Depositories (NSDL and CDSL) maintain electronic records of shares
5. DPs are intermediaries between you and the depository
6. Banks facilitate fund transfers between your accounts
7. Clearing corporations ensure smooth settlement of trades
8. Various charges apply: AMC, brokerage, DP charges, and statutory charges
9. Trading account is for transactions; Demat account is for holding shares
